# What the published evidence actually says

**This file is not user-facing copy and is not a source for user-facing copy.**
It is the working record behind the claims boundary the app operates inside. If
anything here ends up in front of a user it belongs on a clearly-marked
Learn → Evidence page that describes the literature *including its weaknesses* —
never attached to a benefit as its proof.

Everything below was read from the source abstract. Where a retrieved abstract
was truncated before the number I wanted, that is marked **[abstract truncated]**
and no number is asserted. Nothing here is from memory.

Retrieved 2026-08-17 via PubMed and Crossref. Full-text was not available for any
of these — **all of this is abstract-level.** Effect sizes are quoted as the
authors reported them; none were recomputed.

---

## The short answer

Yes, there are credible studies. No, they do not license the sales copy people
usually want to write from them.

Three findings recur in every serious review, and they need to be carried
together or the picture is dishonest:

1. **Against doing nothing, autogenic training works.** Medium effect sizes,
   replicated, across a wide spread of conditions.
2. **Against other relaxation methods, it is indistinguishable.** Not better.
   Sometimes slightly worse. This is the finding that gets dropped.
3. **The trial quality is poor.** Small samples, weak controls, and — repeatedly
   noted — trials that were not actually testing standard AT.

There is also a fourth thing worth saying plainly: **no study has tested an app.**
The evidence base is built almost entirely on eight-week courses taught by a
person. The one RCT that delivered AT as an audio recording had 22 participants
and found improvement in subjective sleep quality with no objective change.

## The anchor studies

### Stetter & Kupper 2002 — the meta-analysis everything else cites
*Appl Psychophysiol Biofeedback 2002;27(1):45–98 · doi:10.1023/a:1014576505223 · PMID 12001885*

73 controlled outcome studies published 1952–1999; 60 qualified, of which 35 were
RCTs. Reported:

- Medium-to-large effect sizes for pre-post disease-specific effects, larger in
  the RCTs.
- **Medium effect sizes against real control conditions.**
- **Against other psychological treatment: mostly no effect, or small *negative*
  effect sizes.**
- The pattern held at follow-up.
- "Unspecific" effects — mood, cognitive performance, quality of life,
  physiological variables — *tended to be larger than the main effects.*
- Medium-range positive effects, on ≥3 studies each, for: tension
  headache/migraine, mild-to-moderate essential hypertension, coronary heart
  disease, asthma, somatoform pain disorder, Raynaud's disease, **anxiety
  disorders**, mild-to-moderate depression/dysthymia, and **functional sleep
  disorders**.

This is the single strongest source for the method. It is also 24 years old, and
its evidence stops in 1999.

### Linden 1994 — the earlier quantitative review
*Biofeedback Self Regul 1994;19(3):227–64 · doi:10.1007/BF01721069 · PMID 7811786*

Pooled the English and German literature, controlled studies only. Notes prior
positive findings for migraine, **insomnia** and test anxiety, and adds supportive
findings for angina, asthma, childbirth, eczema, hypertension, infertility,
Raynaud's and recovery from myocardial infarction. Reports AT as associated with
medium-sized pre- to post-treatment effects. **[abstract truncated]**

### Ernst & Kanji 2000 — the corrective
*Complement Ther Med 2000;8(2):106–10 · doi:10.1054/ctim.2000.0354 · PMID 10859603*

Eight controlled trials of AT for stress and anxiety. Seven reported positive
effects on stress, one did not. But: "the majority of trials were
methodologically flawed", "deviations from the accepted technique of AT were
conspicuous", and trials using classical AT were in the minority. Conclusion, in
their words: **"No firm conclusions could be drawn."**

Two of the same authors went on to run the nursing-students RCT below, which
suggests they meant it as a call for better trials rather than a dismissal.

## By outcome

### Anxiety and stress

**Seo & Kim 2019** — systematic review and meta-analysis
*J Korean Acad Nurs 2019;49(4):361–374 · doi:10.4040/jkan.2019.49.4.361 · PMID 31477667*

21 studies reviewed, 11 meta-analysed.
- Anxiety: **SMD −1.37 (95% CI −2.07 to −0.67), n = 85** — short-term
  intervention, healthy adults. A large effect, but on a very small pooled sample
  with a wide interval.
- Depression: **SMD −0.29 (95% CI −0.50 to −0.07), n = 327** — long-term
  intervention, patient groups. A small effect, barely clearing zero.
- Increased the high-frequency component of heart rate variability.

Note the shape of that: the big effect is the small sample, the big sample gives
the small effect. That is what you would expect if the literature is
publication-biased.

**Kanji, White & Ernst 2006** — the best-designed AT anxiety RCT
*J Adv Nurs 2006;53(6):729–35 · doi:10.1111/j.1365-2648.2006.03779.x · PMID 16553681*

93 nursing students, three arms: 8 weekly AT sessions vs 8 weekly laughter therapy
(an *attention* control — this is what makes the trial good) vs no intervention.

- Greater reduction in State (P<0.001) and Trait (P<0.001) anxiety than **both**
  other groups, immediately after treatment.
- Greater reduction in systolic (P<0.01) and diastolic (P<0.05) blood pressure and
  pulse rate (P<0.002).
- **No difference on the Maslach Burnout Inventory.**
- Authors' conclusion: "at least a **short-term** effect".

Follow-up ran to 11 months; the abstract reports the significant results at
end-of-treatment only, which is itself informative.

**Manzoni et al. 2008** — relaxation training generally
*BMC Psychiatry 2008;8:41 · doi:10.1186/1471-244X-8-41 · PMID 18518981*

27 studies, 1997–2007. Cohen's **d = .57 (95% CI .52–.68)** within-group,
**d = .51 (95% CI .46–.634)** between-group. Efficacy was **higher for
meditation**, higher among volunteers, and higher for longer treatments.

Important caveat for our purposes: this pools progressive relaxation, autogenic
training, applied relaxation and meditation. It is evidence for the *category*,
and its own subgroup finding points away from AT.

### Sleep

This is the weakest link relative to how prominently we feature it.

**Bowden, Lorenc & Robinson 2012** — prospective cohort, no control group
*Prim Health Care Res Dev 2012;13(2):175–85 · doi:10.1017/S1463423611000181 · PMID 21787446*

153 patients completed a standard 8-week AT course at the Royal London Hospital
for Integrated Medicine (UCLH NHS Foundation Trust); 73% had a sleep-related
problem. Improvements in sleep onset latency (P=0.049), falling asleep faster
after night waking (P<0.001), feeling more refreshed (P<0.001) and more energised
on waking (P=0.019). MYMOP symptom and well-being scores, and anxiety and
depression, all improved (P<0.001).

The interesting detail: **sleep was not a focus of the training.** The authors
note improvements occurred "despite, or possibly due to, not focusing on sleep".
That is exactly the app's stance — we do not sell sleep as the target.

The fatal caveat: **pre-post cohort, no control.** Regression to the mean,
expectancy and the eight weeks of clinical attention are all uncontrolled.

**Litwic-Kamińska et al. 2022** — the only audio-recording RCT
*Int J Environ Res Public Health 2022;19(23):16043 · doi:10.3390/ijerph192316043 · PMID 36498114*

University athletes with reduced sleep quality, randomised to 14 days of an AT
audio recording (n=11) vs background music (n=11), with actigraphy and daily logs.

- **Actigraphy sleep parameters, daily activity and physiological reactivity did
  not differ between groups.**
- PSQI — subjective sleep quality — significantly improved in the AT group.

This is the closest study in the literature to what this app actually is:
recorded delivery, ~two weeks, self-directed. It found a subjective improvement
and no objective one, at n=22. It is a pilot and the authors say so.

**Ramirez-Garcia et al. 2023** — RCT in people living with HIV
*Complement Ther Clin Pract 2023;50:101716 · doi:10.1016/j.ctcp.2022.101716 · PMID 36528982*

AT (n=32) vs control (n=31). Significant improvement in sleep for AT participants
immediately post-intervention. Social and mental quality-of-life dimensions
improved in **both** groups at three months.

### Pain and headache

**Kohlert, Wick & Rosendahl 2022** — the most recent good meta-analysis
*Int J Behav Med 2022;29(5):531–542 · doi:10.1007/s12529-021-10038-6 · PMID 34705227*

13 RCTs, 15 comparisons, 576 participants, chronic pain.
- **vs passive control: g = 0.58 (95% CI 0.36–0.79), k=9, I² = 0%** — moderate,
  and strikingly consistent.
- **vs other psychological interventions: g = −0.05 (95% CI −0.30 to 0.20), k=6,
  I² = 0%** — nothing.
- Risk of bias "some concerns" in the majority of studies.

The cleanest single illustration of the whole picture: real against nothing,
identical to the alternatives.

**Kanji, White & Ernst 2006** — tension-type headache
*Complement Ther Med 2006;14(2):144–50 · doi:10.1016/j.ctim.2006.03.001 · PMID 16765853*

Seven controlled trials, low methodological quality. None showed AT convincingly
superior. Some suggested it is **no different from hypnosis and inferior to
biofeedback**. No consistent evidence of superiority.

**Seo, Hong, Choi & Kim 2018** — headache
*Complement Ther Med 2018;39:62–67 · doi:10.1016/j.ctim.2018.05.005 · PMID 30012394*

Six RCTs; five showed statistically significant headache reduction from AT-only or
biofeedback-assisted AT. But the variation in intervention, training length and
outcome measures "did not facilitate rigorous evaluation".

### Blood pressure

**Aivazyan, Zaitsev & Yurenev 1988** — five-year randomised follow-up
*Health Psychol 1988;7 Suppl:201–8 · PMID 2977328*

90 patients with essential hypertension, randomised (AT n=44, control n=46),
followed 5 years. AT group: BP reduced 5.8 systolic / 3.2 diastolic vs 4.3 / 2.0;
smaller increase in left-ventricular myocardial mass (**14.6 g vs 38.2 g**);
improved psychological indices; fewer sick days. More effective in mild than
moderate hypertension.

The longest follow-up in the AT literature. Also 38 years old, and the blood
pressure difference between arms is under 2 mmHg.

The Watanabe chronobiometric series (PMID 9017758, 14596365) reports reduced
circadian *variability* of systolic BP; the authors themselves call the results
"provisional statistics, the utility of which depends on replication", and a
published comment (PMID 9229439) raises methodological objections.

**Do not put blood pressure anywhere near user-facing copy.** It is a medical
claim under any reading, regardless of what the studies say.

### Children and adolescents

**Goldbeck & Schmid 2003**
*J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry 2003;42(9):1046–54 · doi:10.1097/01.CHI.0000070244.24125.F · PMID 12960704*

50 children and adolescents (mean 10.2y, range 6–15), 15 randomised to a waiting
list. Parent-reported CBCL symptoms reduced vs control (**ES 0.49**); complaint
list **ES 0.36**. But **child-reported** stress and psychosomatic complaints
improved in intervention *and* control alike, with no group × time interaction —
i.e. the effect is visible to parents and not to the children themselves.

### Pregnancy — a Cochrane review exists, and it is thin

**Marc, Toureche, Ernst et al. 2011**
*Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2011;(7):CD007559 · doi:10.1002/14651858.CD007559.pub2 · PMID 21735413*

Mind-body interventions for anxiety in pregnancy: 8 trials, 556 participants, of
which **exactly one** studied autogenic training. No meta-analysis was possible.
**[abstract truncated]** at the sentence beginning "Autogenic training might be
effective for decreasing…", so no result is asserted here.

## What the evidence does *not* support

- **That AT beats other relaxation methods.** Three independent sources say the
  opposite: Stetter 2002 (small negative effect sizes vs other psychological
  treatment), Kohlert 2022 (g = −0.05), Kanji 2006 headache (no different from
  hypnosis, inferior to biofeedback). Manzoni 2008 found meditation outperformed
  the category average.
- **That an app works.** Nothing tests app delivery. The nearest evidence is one
  n=22 pilot of an audio recording.
- **Anything about the 9-step ladder, the gate, or the 90-second dose as
  implemented here.** Those are our design decisions, not findings.
- **Burnout.** Explicitly null in the best-controlled trial (Kanji 2006).
- **Cognitive performance as a headline.** Stetter 2002 lists it among
  "unspecific effects" that were larger than the main effects — but that is an
  aggregate observation about a category, not a measured cognitive outcome, and
  it cannot carry a "sharper thinking" claim. This is consistent with the
  decision already taken to rule out brain-performance framing.

### Where the app's current copy stands against this

Mostly fine, with one exception.

| Current claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "Less noise in your head — not silence, the volume down" | Experiential, unfalsifiable, no study needed. Fine. |
| "Coming down faster after something stressful" | Consistent with the anxiety/stress literature; phrased experientially. Fine. |
| "A clearer head afterwards" | Fine as phrased — it does not claim measured cognition. |
| "A trained physical response, not a mood" | Consistent with Stetter's "psychophysiological determined relaxation response". Fine. |
| 90 seconds × 3 a day | Three-times-daily practice is the pattern used in the literature (e.g. Watanabe, PMID 9017758, "practiced three times daily"). The 90-second length is ours. Fine, and not a health claim. |
| **"Getting to sleep. *The most common reason people keep it up.*"** | **This one is a factual assertion about users and we have no data for it.** It is not a health claim, so it is not a legal problem — but it is an unsourced statement of fact. Either soften it or drop the superlative. |

The eight-week course length that runs through nearly every trial (Kanji 2006,
Bowden 2012, Asbury 2009, Ramirez-Garcia 2023) is worth noting: our nine-step
ladder at roughly a week a step lands in the same territory by accident of
following the traditional protocol. That is a coincidence, not a validation, and
should not be presented as one.

## Safety and adverse effects

The AT literature does **not** systematically report harms. Stetter 2002 and
Kohlert 2022 report efficacy without a harms analysis; the 2020 protocol by
Ramirez-Garcia et al. (PMID 32264955) lists adverse effects as an outcome
precisely because they are under-reported. Absence of reported harm here is
absence of reporting, not evidence of safety.

What *is* documented, in the wider relaxation literature:

- **Relaxation-induced anxiety** is real and named. Heide & Borkovec,
  *J Consult Clin Psychol* 1983;51(2):171 (doi:10.1037/0022-006x.51.2.171) —
  paradoxical anxiety enhancement during relaxation training. Replicated and
  extended by Kim & Newman, *J Affect Disord* 2019
  (doi:10.1016/j.jad.2019.08.045).

This supports keeping the "if restlessness or emotion comes up" panel, and
supports the current framing of it as expected rather than alarming. It also
means the panel should stay in the mandatory-tap onboarding rather than being
demoted to Learn.

The light-headedness caution and the take-back instruction are traditional
protocol, not trial findings, and are correctly presented as such.

## On the founder's name

Independent of the copy-simplification reason for removing it, there is a
documented ethical one.

**Brunner, Schrempf & Steger 2008**, "Johannes Heinrich Schultz and National
Socialism", *Isr J Psychiatry Relat Sci* 2008;45(4):257–62 · PMID 19439831.

Schultz was assistant director of the Göring Institute from 1936 to 1945. The
paper documents that he publicly advocated compulsory sterilization and the
"annihilation of life unworthy of life"; that he developed a diagnostic scheme
distinguishing curable from "hereditary/incurable" which was then used to decide
between life and death; and that he carried out degrading "treatments" on
homosexual concentration-camp prisoners who were in mortal danger.

The name is already out of the explainer and out of Learn. This is the reason it
should stay out, and it is a stronger reason than the one recorded in commit
`60bf3e1`. The method's 1920s Berlin origin and the take-the-hypnotist-out-of-
hypnosis framing remain accurate and are unaffected.

## Position on citing any of this in the app

Unchanged from the position taken twice before, and the evidence above reinforces
rather than weakens it:

**Citing a study next to a benefit converts a description into a claim.** "Less
noise in your head" is an invitation to notice something. "Less noise in your
head¹" with a footnote to Stetter 2002 is an assertion that a named body of
research predicts that outcome for this user, from this app — and no study in
this file supports that, because none of them tested this app.

The defensible use of this material is a Learn → Evidence page that:

- describes what the research base is, **including** that AT is not better than
  other relaxation methods and that the trials are of poor quality;
- states plainly that no study has tested this app or any app;
- links out rather than paraphrasing findings into promises;
- carries no numbers next to benefit language anywhere else in the product.

That page would make the product *more* honest than it is now, not less. But it
must not be used to upgrade the tone of the benefit copy — the copy stays
experiential either way.

Blood pressure, heart rate variability, hypertension, Raynaud's, asthma, coronary
heart disease and pain should not appear on such a page at all. They are the
findings most likely to be read as medical claims and they buy nothing with this
audience.

## Method and limits

- Sources located via Crossref (`api.crossref.org`) and PubMed search HTML.
  NCBI E-utilities and the Europe PMC REST API were unreachable from this
  environment, so retrieval was by scraping PubMed's `format=abstract` results
  pages, rate-limited.
- **Abstract-level only.** No full text was read. Effect sizes, confidence
  intervals and p-values are as printed in the abstracts.
- No independent assessment of risk of bias was made beyond what the authors
  themselves reported.
- Non-English and older German-language literature is substantially
  under-represented here; Linden 1994 notes it is a significant portion of the
  field.
- Searches covered: AT meta-analyses and systematic reviews; AT with anxiety,
  sleep/insomnia, depression, pain, headache, blood pressure, children and
  adolescents, cognition/attention; relaxation-training meta-analyses; and
  relaxation adverse effects. Retrieved 2026-08-17.
