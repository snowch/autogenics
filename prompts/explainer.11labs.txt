Autogenic training was developed in Berlin in the nineteen twenties by a doctor called Johannes Schultz. "Autogenic" means generated from within, and that is the whole idea. You are not being relaxed by me, or by a recording. You are learning to produce the state yourself.

[pause 1.5s]

There are six standard exercises. The first, and the only one you should touch for now, is heaviness in the arms.

[pause 1.5s]

Here is why heaviness. When your body shifts out of alert mode, your muscles release the low background tension they hold all day. Heaviness is what that release feels like from the inside. So the sensation isn't really the goal — it's the signal that the shift has happened.

[pause 1.5s]

What you'll actually do is repeat one short phrase: my right arm is heavy.

[pause 1s]

Say it silently, inside your own head, in your own voice. Not aloud. Four to six times, slowly, and then let it go.

[pause 1.5s]

Now the part that catches everyone.

[pause 1s]

You cannot make this happen by trying. Effort is the one thing that reliably blocks it. If you concentrate hard on your arm and demand that it feel heavy, it won't.

[pause 1s]

It's like falling asleep. You can arrange the conditions, but you can't force the moment. So hold the phrase lightly, notice whatever is actually there, and let it be uninteresting. This is called passive concentration, and it's the whole skill.

[pause 2s]

What should you expect? Probably nothing at all for the first few days. That's normal, and it isn't failure.

[pause 1s]

Heaviness usually appears somewhere between a few days and two weeks of regular practice. It may not arrive as heaviness exactly — people describe warmth, tingling, sinking, or the arm feeling larger, or further away than it is. Any of those count.

[pause 1.5s]

Sometimes you'll get the opposite: restlessness, a twitch, an urge to move, or unexpected emotion. That's known as an autogenic discharge. It's a recognised part of the process, not a sign that something is wrong. If it's uncomfortable, open your eyes, move your arms and legs, and stop there for that session.

How often. This matters more than anything else I've said.

[pause 1s]

Short and frequent beats long and occasional. Aim for about ninety seconds, three times a day — morning, midday, and evening. Frequency is what builds the response. One long session a week will not, however diligently you sit through it.

Every practice ends the same way, and this part is not optional.

[pause 1s]

Bend and stretch your arms firmly, fists clenched, several times. Take two deep breaths. Then open your eyes. This returns your muscle tone and circulation to normal. Skip it and you can be left groggy or heavy-headed for a while afterwards.

[pause 1s]

The one exception is if you're practising in bed to fall asleep. In that case, don't do it — just stay lying down and let yourself drift off.

Two cautions before you start. Don't practise while driving, or doing anything else that needs your attention. And deep relaxation can leave you a little light-headed when you stand up — so if you have low blood pressure, a heart condition, diabetes, or epilepsy, speak to your doctor before making this a daily habit.
